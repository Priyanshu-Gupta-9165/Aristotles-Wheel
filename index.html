Mechanics · Problem XXIV · c. 300 BCE

The smaller circle should fall behind. It never does.

Bind a small circle inside a greater one and roll the pair through a single turn: both sweep the same road, though their edges disagree by 2π(R−r). Aristotle noticed. Twenty-three centuries of geometers argued. This page rolls the thing out in front of you.

§01
◈ §01 — The Claim

The same road, two edges.

Roll exactly one turn. Mark the road at departure and arrival.

The outer circle, unrolled edge-first, fills that span to the millimetre — that is what circumference means.

The inner circle, unrolled beside it, falls short. And yet, while riding, it crossed every point of the very same span. Two unequal edges, one road, no remainder.

The absurdity — so the Mechanica leaves it.

Phase 01 · Construction

One axle, two circles.

Fix two circles, concentric, on a single axle. Call the outer radius R and the inner r. One body — but two edges, and soon, two stories.

watch — the dimensions R and r
Phase 02 · Contact

The road belongs to the greater.

Set the wheel down: only the outer circle touches. Its contact point is instantaneously at rest — that is the whole definition of pure rolling, and it fixes the pace: x = Rθ. Everything else on the axle is a passenger.

watch — the copper contact point, always still
Phase 03 · The Passenger

The lesser circle rides; it does not roll.

The inner point draws a curtate cycloid — a flattened wave. Its lowest point never stops moving: it slides forward at (R−r)ω. Motion where contact ought to be still is slip, by definition.

watch — the vermilion slip vector, riding along
Phase 04 · Accounting

The missing length hides in plain sight.

Per revolution both circles sweep the same span 2πR — but the inner edge pays only 2πr. The shortfall 2π(R−r) is not lost; it is smeared across the whole span as slip. Aristotle saw two equal lines. Galileo saw that one of them is mostly emptiness.

§03
◈ §03 — Polygonize It

Galileo's sharp wheel.

In 1638 Galileo replaced the smooth circles with nested polygons — and the slip grew corners you can see. Every lurch leaves a visible void in the lesser wheel's line. Multiply the sides: the clunks soften to a glide, the voids multiply without bound and shrink beyond sight. His answer: the small circle equals the great line only because it is riddled with infinitely many empty points.

§04
◈ §04 — The Resolution

Three constraints dissolve it.

i

One road, one master.

The ground touches the outer circle alone, and at that point the wheel is instantaneously still. A single kinematic constraint sets the pace of the entire body — the inner circle never negotiates with the road at all.

x = Rθ · nothing else decides the distance
ii

The passenger slides.

Carried at Rω by the axle, spun backward at rω by the rotation, the inner circle's lowest point still creeps forward at (R−r)ω. If it had its own road, it would skid along it — continuously, silently, for the whole journey.

vcontact = Rω − rω = (R−r)ω ≠ 0 → slip
iii

Equal lengths, unequal contents.

The lesser circle truly does traverse the greater line — but not by rolling alone: its own 2πr of edge, plus 2π(R−r) of distributed slip. Galileo phrased it as arcs and voids, three centuries before measure theory would shrug and confirm him: length is blind to holes of measure zero.

2πR = 2πr + 2π(R−r) · full line = edge + voids
§05
◈ §05 — Marginalia

Readers across twenty-three centuries.

c. 300 BCE

Mechanica — Problem 24

The puzzle enters the record in the Aristotelian corpus (likely Straton's hand): when the wheel moves, the inner circle seems to traverse a path equal to the outer — how? It is posed, puzzled over, and left standing.

1638

Galileo — Two New Sciences

Galileo swaps circles for nested polygons, counts the gaps, and dares the limit: the smaller circle's line equals the greater's only because it is full of "infinitely many voids." Infinity, handled without flinching.

1639 – 1658

The Cycloid Wars

Roberval names the curve traced by the outer point; Mersenne mails challenges across Europe; Torricelli, Pascal and Wallis answer. The humble trace of Aristotle's wheel becomes the most contested curve in mathematics.

1659

Huygens Bends the Curve

Huygens hangs a pendulum between cycloidal cheeks so a clock beats truly. The paradox's byproduct becomes engineering — rolling curves now keep time.

Today

Kinematics Settles It

Rolling is a velocity condition: the contact point must be instantaneously still. Only the outer circle satisfies it; the inner slips at (R−r)ω. The paradox was never about geometry alone — it was a dynamics problem wearing a geometry costume.

Q.E.D. · Quod Erat Demonstrandum

The wheel is reconciled: equal roads, unequal rolling.
